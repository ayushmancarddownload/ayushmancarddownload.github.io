Building a budget sounds simple until you actually sit down and try to make the numbers match your life. This guide walks through a method that holds up past the first week.

## Start with real numbers, not guesses

Pull your last two months of bank and card statements before you write a single figure down. Most budgets fail in the first month because they're built on what people assume they spend rather than what they actually spend.

Sort every transaction into a small number of buckets:

- Fixed costs (rent, insurance, subscriptions)
- Variable needs (groceries, fuel, utilities)
- Discretionary spending (dining out, entertainment)
- Savings and debt payments

## Give every rupee, dollar, or euro a job

Once you know your real numbers, assign every unit of income to a category before the month begins. This is often called zero-based budgeting, and it works because it forces a decision up front instead of leaving money "unassigned" and easy to lose track of.

> A budget is not a restriction — it's a plan for money you've already decided matters to you.

## Build in a buffer for the month you didn't expect

Set aside a small miscellaneous category, even 3-5% of your income, for the expenses that don't fit anywhere else: a birthday gift, a car repair, a higher-than-usual electricity bill. Without this buffer, one unusual week can make the whole plan feel like it failed.

## Review weekly, not just monthly

A ten-minute check-in each week catches overspending while it's still small. Waiting until the end of the month to look at your numbers means you find out too late to adjust.

## Adjust the categories, not the habit

If you consistently overspend in one bucket, the fix is usually to resize the bucket to match reality, not to abandon budgeting altogether. A budget is a living document that should reflect how you actually live, refined a little every month.

Start this week with just one month of real data, and refine from there.
